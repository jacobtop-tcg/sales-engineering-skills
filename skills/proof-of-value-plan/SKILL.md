# Proof of Value plan

A Proof of Concept tests whether the product works in the customer's environment. A Proof of Value tests whether the product delivers the business outcomes the economic buyer has agreed to, and that agreement comes before the proof starts.

## Ask for these inputs

1. The deal, the product being evaluated and the economic buyer.
2. The business outcomes the customer has stated, in their words, and who said each.
3. The capabilities and use cases the team plans to test, and who at the customer will judge the result.
4. The pilot, trial or evaluation agreement, plus any completed order form, schedule or project plan.
5. The customer's decision date and the pilot's planned end date.

## Steps

1. Agree the outcomes first. Record two to four business outcomes in the customer's own words, agreed by the economic buyer. If the team started from product capabilities, flag it: that route can produce an impressive evaluation that never answers the customer's business question.
2. Work back to the test. With the customer, list the minimum capabilities needed to reach each outcome, the use cases that demonstrate them, and the criteria for judging the result. Ask about the work itself: for faster invoice approval, where invoices wait today, which exceptions cause delays and who must act on them. Those findings decide which parts of the customer's work belong in the proof.
3. Write the success criteria with the people who will judge the result. For each, state what the result is compared with and what evidence the proof can reasonably produce. Mark any effect that depends on wider implementation or on changes to the customer's process before anyone promises to demonstrate it.
4. Hold the go or no-go. Before technical validation, the account executive and the sales engineer confirm the outcomes, the timeline and the next step with the economic buyer. If nobody can agree what the proof is meant to establish, go back to discovery. A Business Value Assessment can help when the answer needs conversations with several of the customer's people. More time in a test environment will not settle a business decision nobody has made.
5. Make the step after a successful proof explicit. The customer may still need to compare commercial terms, get another approval or plan the implementation.
6. Check the agreement:
   - Does access end at the close of the period, or does a subscription start automatically? For an automatic transition, is there a right to stop it and a notice deadline?
   - What happens to the customer's data at the end?
   - Do the terms limit disclosing results or comparisons? Check before results go into a report that leaves the company.
   - On the buyer's own proof of concept paper, which ticked policies bind the proof itself?
   - Where are the success result, the decision-maker and the decision date written? Common Paper's published pilot terms, for example, do not say. Check the completed order form, schedule or project plan before treating them as missing.
7. Keep the buyer's decision date separate from the pilot's end date. If continued access is needed, letting the pilot end before the decision may leave a gap.
8. Put the customer's steps toward the decision, each with an owner and a date, into the mutual action plan.
9. Plan one readout for three readers. The economic buyer needs the business case for the agreed outcomes and the investment. The deal champion needs to judge whether the right tool is being put in place and to carry the decision internally. The technical champion needs to understand how the product works, including limitations that matter in their environment. Keep gaps visible: a failed capability or a result that depends on a change still to be made belongs in the decision whether to proceed.
10. After the customer decides, the value presentation and the success criteria become part of the customer's success plan. Customer Success and the sales engineer prepare that handover together.

## Output

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Business outcomes (2 to 4) | Customer's words, who said it, economic buyer agreement and date |
| Capabilities and use cases | Each linked to an outcome and to the part of the customer's work it covers |
| Success criteria | Criterion, compared with what, evidence the proof can produce, who judges it |
| Dependencies | Effects that need wider implementation or process change |
| Go or no-go | Date; outcomes, timeline and step after a successful proof confirmed with the economic buyer |
| Agreement check | End mechanism, notice deadline, data at end, limits on results, buyer policies |
| Dates | Pilot end and buyer's decision date, separately |
| Readout and handover | Needs of each of the three readers; Customer Success owner |

## Checks

- An outcome phrased as a product feature, or in the vendor's words, is weak. Send it back.
- An outcome the economic buyer has not agreed is a hypothesis. Do not start technical validation on it.
- Criteria count when the people who will judge the result have agreed them.
- If the pilot end date is the only date in the plan, the decision date is missing.
- If the team reports on its proofs, keep technical wins apart from deals won.
