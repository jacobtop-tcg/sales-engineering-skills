# Business Value Assessment interviews

A Business Value Assessment uses stakeholder interviews to establish what a proposed software purchase needs to achieve. The interviews cover the customer's current tools and problems, the state they want to reach, the capabilities that would require and how they would judge success. The output is a business case, a roadmap and a more focused set of criteria for a Proof of Value.

The reason for interviewing several people: the people involved can be describing different problems even when they appear to agree on the solution. A case built with one person can miss work, consequences and objections that become obvious when you speak to the people around them.

## Ask for these inputs

1. The proposed purchase and the goals stated so far, with who stated each.
2. Whether the customer already understands the problem, has credible figures and knows who can decide.
3. Who the economic buyer is, and whether they have agreed what would make the investment worthwhile.
4. Which departments carry the benefits and which carry the work.
5. Whether a proof is about to be scoped, and what business outcome it is meant to demonstrate.

## Step 1: Decide whether the interviews are worth it

An assessment of this kind is a substantial commitment from the customer. It has to give them something they could not get from a quick conversation about return on investment.

- If the customer already understands the problem, has credible figures and knows who can decide, recommend a shorter working session.
- Recommend the fuller interview set when important parts of the decision are still spread across the organization: the benefits sit in one department and the work in another, the economic buyer has not agreed what would make the investment worthwhile, or the team is about to scope a proof without knowing which business outcome it must demonstrate.

Do not size the interview count by what the method allows. Choose the people who can answer the open questions. The count follows the work needed to understand the decision.

## Step 2: Plan the interview list

For each interviewee, record the role, the open question they can answer and why their view might differ from others. Include the economic buyer, who must agree which business outcomes the investment is meant to deliver, and the people whose work will change, who help establish whether those outcomes are realistic and how to measure them.

## Step 3: Ask about the work

Goals that sound compatible can still pull the work in different directions. Investigate how they fit. Ask people to take you through the work:

- Where does a case wait?
- What gets entered twice?
- Who picks it up when the first person cannot resolve it?
- Which part would actually change if the company bought the product?

Look for consequences a vendor's calculation of time saved would miss: faster handling in one team moving follow-up work to another department, or removing a system that a team relies on.

## Step 4: Treat numbers and disagreements as findings

- For every number, record which task it refers to, how often it happens and what the estimate is based on. Keep estimates labelled as estimates all the way into the business case.
- When a manager describes the intended process and someone doing the work describes what happens when information is missing, record both. The difference can change the scope or the value attributed to the project.
- Allow the assessment to expose a problem with the proposed solution. If that outcome is ruled out, you are asking the customer to justify a conclusion already reached.

## Step 5: Write the outputs

- Business case: the proposed investment and its assumptions, written so the customer's people can challenge the assumptions and still understand why the investment is proposed. Do not fill it with supportive quotations.
- Roadmap: the changes needed to reach the desired state.
- Proof of Value criteria: the capabilities and outcomes that need demonstrating before the customer can decide, chosen before the sales engineer starts building the environment.

If the interviews leave the business outcome unresolved, record it as an open decision and take it back to the customer. Starting the proof will not settle it.

## Output template

| Interviewee (role) | Open question they answer | What they described | Numbers given (task, frequency, basis) | Differs from | Effect on scope or value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Followed by: agreed business outcomes (with economic buyer confirmation), assumptions list, roadmap changes, Proof of Value criteria, open decisions.

## Checks

- A case built from one person's account is weak.
- A number with no task, frequency or basis is unsupported.
- An outcome the economic buyer has not agreed is unresolved.
- Proof criteria that do not trace to an agreed outcome should be questioned.
